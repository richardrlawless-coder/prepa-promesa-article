Three Federal Courts Are Now Examining Pieces of a Record That Could Matter to a Future RICO Investigation

The courts are not deciding whether a racketeering crime occurred. But their rulings on government knowledge, allegedly misleading representations, materiality, and resulting judicial consequences could determine whether a broader investigation has a factual foundation.

By Richard Lawless · SEC Dodd-Frank Whistleblower · August 15, 2026

These court actions relate to approximately $75 billion in municipal bonds sold by Wall Street banks and approximately $90 billion in taxpayer-funded bailouts allegedly used to mitigate the consequences of defaulted bonds.

New evidence uncovered through Freedom of Information requests is cited in this report as indicating that the United States Government made as many as 39 challenged statements across ten federal court cases that allegedly contributed to dismissals largely on procedural grounds.

The newly released materials are also cited as confirming the existence of large volumes of SEC, DOJ, and Treasury records concerning investigative activity, whistleblower processing, interagency communications, and possible bond-related misconduct. According to the article's evidentiary record, those agencies possessed relevant information before, during, and after congressional consideration of PROMESA.

Important qualification: None of the allegations in this article establishes a criminal violation. Questions involving intent, materiality, causation, conspiracy, obstruction, retaliation, or racketeering would require factual findings by a court, Congress, an inspector general, or appropriate law-enforcement authorities.

The central question is whether the later-released records materially alter the factual picture presented in prior litigation and whether they warrant further judicial, congressional, inspector-general, or law-enforcement examination.

One theory raised in the underlying materials is that powerful financial and political interests may have influenced governmental decisions. That theory remains unproven. The evidence currently supports investigation of that question, not a factual conclusion that such pressure occurred.

The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals, the First Circuit Court of Appeals, and federal courts in California have been asked to consider filings that raise issues involving government knowledge, prior representations, materiality, and litigation consequences. Congressional oversight bodies have also been provided with FOIA materials and related reports.

Federal criminal provisions identified for investigative screening

For purposes of issue-spotting, the Curtin evidence report identifies roughly ten federal criminal provisions that could merit investigative screening if supported by the required elements and evidence:

  1. 18 U.S.C. § 1621 — Perjury. Potential relevance depends on whether a materially false statement was knowingly and willfully made under penalty of perjury.
  2. 18 U.S.C. § 1623 — False declarations before a federal court. Application would depend on the precise declaration, oath mechanism, materiality, and knowledge.
  3. 18 U.S.C. § 1622 — Subornation of perjury. This would require evidence that a person procured another to commit perjury; the current record does not itself establish that procurement.
  4. 18 U.S.C. § 1503 — Obstruction of justice. Potential relevance would turn on proof that conduct was undertaken corruptly to influence a federal judicial proceeding.
  5. 18 U.S.C. § 1512(c) — Obstruction of an official proceeding. Screening may be warranted if evidence supports corrupt concealment, destruction, alteration, or other obstructive conduct tied to an official proceeding.
  6. 18 U.S.C. § 1519 — Falsification or concealment of records. This is narrower than ordinary nondisclosure and requires the specific statutory intent and conduct.
  7. 18 U.S.C. § 1513 — Retaliation against an informant or witness. The later records identify whistleblower processing, but retaliation would require proof that an adverse act was intentionally taken because truthful information was supplied to law enforcement.
  8. 18 U.S.C. § 371 — Conspiracy to commit an offense or defraud the United States. Interagency coordination alone is not conspiracy; an unlawful agreement would still have to be proven.
  9. 18 U.S.C. § 1001 — False statements or concealment in federal matters. Potential non-court applications may exist, subject to the statute's limitations and judicial-proceeding exception.
  10. 18 U.S.C. §§ 241/242 — Conspiracy against rights / deprivation of rights under color of law. These are highly contingent and would require proof of intentional deprivation of a federally protected right.

Two factual discrepancies that merit objective review

The Katilius declaration

The report identifies a declaration stating there were no responsive records for FOIA Request 22-00015-FOPA, while later SEC processing reportedly identified approximately 330 email records and produced 106 responsive pages. That discrepancy does not by itself establish a false sworn statement; search scope, timing, custodians, methodology, and later-discovered records could matter. It is, however, an objectively testable discrepancy.

The Green declaration

The report also focuses on a declaration made under penalty of perjury concerning the nature of related litigation. The later Curtin records are cited as documenting whistleblower processing, investigative work items, substantive analysis, and SEC-DOJ review. Whether any earlier declaration was materially false would require a comparison of the exact statement, the declarant's knowledge, context, and the legal elements of the relevant statute.

Potential RICO analysis

Federal RICO under 18 U.S.C. § 1962 requires, among other things, an enterprise, a pattern of racketeering activity, qualifying predicate acts, and the required relationship between the enterprise and the acts. A pattern requires qualifying acts that are related and amount to or threaten continued criminal activity.

The present materials can be framed for investigative screening around four questions: whether an association-in-fact enterprise existed; whether qualifying predicate acts occurred; whether the conduct showed relatedness and continuity; and whether there was a common unlawful purpose.

The strongest current evidence concerns the existence of investigative activity, whistleblower processing, interagency communications, and a chronology spanning bond issuance, federal review, PROMESA-era activity, restructuring, and later litigation.

The missing bridge is still critical: parallel institutional conduct is not the same thing as proof of an agreement or common unlawful purpose.

That distinction is why a RICO-conspiracy question can be described as potentially investigable without saying a RICO violation has been established. Existing communications showing government agencies working together are not themselves evidence that the agencies later agreed to conceal information or commit a criminal offense.

What further investigation would need to determine

Further inquiry would need to examine internal communications, meeting records, document-routing histories, instructions, testimony, custodial searches, agency decision records, and the precise statements made in the relevant litigation. It would also need to separate lawful interagency coordination from any evidence of intentional concealment, obstruction, retaliation, fraud, or an unlawful agreement.

A more precise investigative formulation is:

Whether some subset of financial, governmental, regulatory, restructuring, and litigation actors participated over time in an association-in-fact whose affairs were conducted through qualifying predicate offenses for the purpose of protecting or preserving bond-related financial and institutional interests, including by preventing material evidence concerning PREPA bond validity and prior governmental knowledge from receiving substantive review.

That proposition is presented as a question for investigation, not as an adjudicated finding.
