Documentary Basis for Investigating Whether Powerful Financial Contributors and Congressional Gatekeepers Shaped PROMESA and Influenced the Federal Government’s Treatment of PREPA-Related Evidence

Prepared August 2026

Evidentiary posture: This report supports an investigative hypothesis. It does not state that political influence, bribery, conspiracy, or agency misconduct has been adjudicated.

Executive Summary

The central allegation examined here is not merely that Wall Street contributed money to politicians who later voted for PROMESA. The more serious theory is that powerful financial institutions with major exposure to Puerto Rico debt had strong economic incentives to preserve a restructuring framework favorable to institutional financial interests; that those institutions and the broader financial sector were major political donors and lobbyists; that congressional leaders and legislative gatekeepers receiving substantial financial-sector support organized and advanced PROMESA; and that Treasury, SEC, and DOJ possessed materially relevant PREPA information that was not fully reflected in the public congressional narrative and was later the subject of disputed representations in federal litigation.

The currently assembled record supports investigation of that theory because the separate pieces are no longer speculative in isolation. Treasury was directly involved in the congressional development of PROMESA. Treasury told the House Natural Resources Committee in February 2016 that it looked forward to working with Congress on a solution and testified on April 13 that it would continue working with committee staff to refine the legislation. The House committee later described Treasury testimony as providing the rationale for PROMESA’s restructuring provisions.

At the same time, records preserved in the user’s evidentiary archive describe a 2013 Treasury-to-SEC analysis addressing PREPA’s financial condition, debt-service coverage, receivables, subsidies, liquidity, and reliance on continued borrowing. Later-produced SEC records document Enforcement Matter B-02949 involving PREPA’s 2013 bonds, whistleblower processing, requests for financial analysis, investigative routing and preservation, and SEC communications with DOJ Antitrust personnel.

The resulting investigative question is therefore concrete: when Treasury officials were helping Congress design PROMESA and publicly characterizing Puerto Rico’s debt crisis, what PREPA-specific information was available inside Treasury, SEC, DOJ, and interagency channels—and was that information transmitted to the congressional officials making the legislative decisions?

Campaign-finance and lobbying records establish motive and access, not corrupt intent. The financial sector spent at extraordinary scale in the 2015–2016 election cycle. Key PROMESA actors, including Speaker Paul Ryan and Senator Charles Schumer, were among the largest financial-sector recipients. Schumer entered the PROMESA period with longstanding donor concentrations from Goldman Sachs, Citigroup, and JPMorgan Chase and also operated substantial Senate fundraising and redistribution structures. SIFMA’s Asset Management Group expressly supported PROMESA, and other financially interested actors engaged in Puerto Rico-related lobbying.

The evidence does not yet establish the final causal bridge: a communication, instruction, agreement, or other proof showing that a congressional actor directed Treasury, SEC, or DOJ to suppress, withhold, mischaracterize, or otherwise manage PREPA-related evidence for the purpose of protecting financial contributors or preserving PROMESA. That missing evidence is precisely what congressional subpoenas, inspector-general review, agency email searches, briefing-book production, and testimony could test.

Accordingly, this report concludes that the political-influence theory has a substantial factual foundation sufficient to warrant focused investigation. It should be presented as an evidence-supported hypothesis of motive, access, governmental knowledge, institutional coordination, legislative opportunity, and potentially protective downstream conduct—not as an already-proved quid pro quo.

The Investigative Theory in One Sentence

Whether powerful financial interests with exposure to Puerto Rico debt used political influence with congressional gatekeepers to shape PROMESA and whether that political environment affected what Treasury, SEC, and DOJ disclosed, investigated, litigated, or communicated concerning PREPA-related evidence.

Campaign contributions are more probative when viewed alongside contemporaneous lobbying and formal advocacy.

5. Treasury Was a Central Participant in the Creation of PROMESA

This is a critical link in the influence theory. Treasury was not simply an agency that commented after Congress acted.

  • On February 25, 2016, Treasury Counselor Antonio Weiss told the House Natural Resources Committee that Treasury looked forward to working with Congress on a responsible solution to Puerto Rico’s crisis.

  • On April 13, 2016, Weiss testified on the PROMESA discussion draft and stated that Treasury looked forward to continuing to work with the Committee and staff to refine the legislation immediately following the hearing.

  • The House Natural Resources Committee’s later report stated that Treasury testimony concerning Puerto Rico’s unsustainable debt and disorderly finances provided the basis for the restructuring mechanisms in Titles III and VI.

  • Secretary Jacob Lew publicly backed the legislation during May and June 2016 and urged Congress to enact the restructuring and oversight framework.

Investigative significance: If Treasury simultaneously possessed or had access to materially different PREPA-specific information—especially information concerning offering disclosures, solvency, debt-service coverage, receivables, subsidies, or possible securities-law violations—then Treasury briefing files and interagency communications become central evidence.

5A. Potential Conflict No. 1 — Secretary Jack Lew and Citigroup/Citibank

Documented relationship. Before returning to government, Jacob J. Lew served as a senior Citigroup executive, including as Managing Director and Chief Operating Officer of Citi Global Wealth Management and later Managing Director and COO of Citi Alternative Investments. He subsequently became Treasury Secretary while Treasury was a principal Administration architect and advocate of the federal Puerto Rico response that became PROMESA.

Schumer–Lew relationship and confirmation sponsorship. The official 2013 Senate Finance Committee record shows Senator Charles Schumer personally introducing Jacob Lew for confirmation as Treasury Secretary. Schumer described Lew as a friend of approximately three decades and endorsed the nomination 'wholeheartedly and without any reservation.' Schumer had also supported Lew's earlier confirmation as OMB Director. President Obama formally selected Lew for Treasury; the records reviewed do not establish that Schumer originated the recommendation. The documented point is that Schumer was a longstanding political ally and prominent Senate sponsor of Lew's elevation to Treasury.

Why the relationship matters to the conflict analysis. Schumer was simultaneously a major recipient of financial-sector political support, with longstanding donor concentrations associated with Goldman Sachs, Citigroup, and JPMorgan Chase. Lew arrived at Treasury after serving as a senior Citigroup executive. Treasury under Lew then became a principal Administration architect and advocate of PROMESA, legislation materially affecting Puerto Rico debt markets in which Citigroup/Citibank had been a recurring PREPA underwriter, lender, and creditor. These facts do not establish that Schumer caused Lew to act for Citi; they identify a relationship and influence pathway that should be tested through communications and ethics records.

Parallel Schumer–White relationship. The same 2013 appointment period contains a parallel documented relationship at the SEC. Schumer formally introduced Mary Jo White at her Senate Banking Committee confirmation hearing and strongly endorsed her nomination. White became SEC Chair on April 10, 2013. Approximately four months later, Treasury personnel transmitted the 22-page PREPA/Puerto Rico communication to SEC personnel. White had come to the SEC from Debevoise & Plimpton, where her disclosed Wall Street clients included JPMorgan Chase. The resulting pattern warrants examination of Schumer's communications with both Lew/Treasury and White/SEC concerning Puerto Rico, PREPA, the underwriting banks, B-02949, whistleblower evidence, and PROMESA.

Citigroup's PREPA exposure was not incidental. PREPA offering documents identify Citigroup repeatedly as an underwriter. The 2012 PREPA Power Revenue Bond offering lists Citigroup among the underwriting syndicate. The 2013A Official Statement identifies Citigroup as an underwriter and separately discloses that Citibank, N.A., a Citigroup affiliate, maintained a $250 million revolving line of credit to PREPA for fuel and purchased power, with $249.1 million outstanding as of June 30, 2013. Independent energy-finance research later reported that Citigroup participated in PREPA's underwriting team for every PREPA bond issuance from 2010 through 2013.

Investigative question. When Treasury under Secretary Lew helped formulate and advocate PROMESA, did Treasury ethics officials, congressional committees, or other decision-makers evaluate or disclose Lew's former senior relationship with a financial institution that had served as a recurring PREPA underwriter, lender, and creditor? The relationship is not proof that Lew acted for Citi; it is a conflict/recusal/disclosure issue that warrants documentary review.

5A-1. Emerging Schumer–Treasury–SEC Appointment and Influence Pattern

The records now identify a recurring institutional pattern rather than a single isolated relationship: Schumer had major Wall Street financial support; Schumer personally and strongly sponsored Lew's Treasury confirmation; Lew had recently been a senior Citigroup executive; Schumer also personally introduced and strongly supported Mary Jo White for SEC Chair; White came from a Wall Street defense practice that included JPMorgan; Treasury and SEC then possessed and exchanged PREPA-related information during 2013; Treasury later led the Administration's PROMESA effort; and Schumer voted for PROMESA. Each link is independently lawful and does not establish corruption. Taken together, however, they create a focused basis for investigating whether political relationships, former-client conflicts, and financial-sector interests affected the handling or disclosure of PREPA evidence.

Records needed to test the pattern. Executive-branch FOIA and inspector-general searches should seek all communications from 2013 through June 2016 between Schumer or his staff and Lew, White, Weiss, Treasury Domestic Finance, SEC leadership, SEC Enforcement, and DOJ concerning Puerto Rico, PREPA, Citigroup, JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, B-02949, Richard Lawless, HR 1049, the 2013 Treasury-SEC email, and PROMESA.

5B. Potential Conflict No. 2 — Antonio Weiss, Stephen Campbell, and Lazard

Antonio Weiss. President Obama nominated Weiss, then Global Head of Investment Banking at Lazard, to become Treasury Under Secretary for Domestic Finance. After substantial Senate opposition focused in part on Wall Street influence and his Lazard background, Weiss withdrew from the Senate-confirmed nomination and instead became Counselor to Secretary Lew, a position that did not require Senate confirmation.

Puerto Rico responsibility. Weiss became a central Treasury official on Puerto Rico. Treasury's April 13, 2016 PROMESA testimony shows Weiss appearing as the Department's principal witness and telling the House Natural Resources Committee that significant progress had been made designing the bill and that Treasury would continue working with the Committee and staff to further refine the legislation.

Second former Lazard banker. Treasury added Stephen Campbell, another former Lazard investment banker, to the Puerto Rico debt-crisis team in June 2015. The evidence presently located does not show that Campbell was himself nominated for and rejected from a Senate-confirmed post; the confirmation controversy applies to Weiss.

Lazard's Puerto Rico financial interests. Contemporaneous 2015 conflict-of-interest research reported that Lazard Asset Management had direct Puerto Rico tax-exempt bond exposure as recently as 2014 and that a Lazard-managed fund-of-funds invested in BlueMountain Capital's Credit Alternatives Master Fund and Pine River Capital's Fixed Income Master Fund. BlueMountain was reported to hold approximately $400 million of Puerto Rico bonds, while Pine River was reported to have purchased Puerto Rico debt as early as 2011. Lazard Asset Management Securities was also registered as a broker-dealer in Puerto Rico. These reports prompted a public recusal campaign against Weiss in September 2015.

Investigative question. What Puerto Rico securities, funds, creditor interests, advisory relationships, or trading exposures did Lazard and its affiliates hold while two former Lazard bankers were working inside Treasury on Puerto Rico policy and Weiss was helping Congress design PROMESA? The answer requires fund holdings, trading records, ethics disclosures, recusal records, and Treasury communications—not inference alone.

5C. Material Omission Question — What Treasury Presented to Congress

The public legislative record. Treasury's public testimony emphasized Puerto Rico's unsustainable debt, lack of market access, creditor litigation, fiscal disorder, and the need for restructuring plus oversight. The House committee report expressly relied on Treasury testimony as support for the restructuring mechanisms ultimately embodied in Titles III and VI.

The omitted-information issue. Richard Lawless reports that he reviewed the PROMESA hearing transcripts and found no disclosure by Secretary Lew, Weiss, or other participants of HR 1049, SEC PREPA Matter B-02949, the SEC investigative subpoenas, the whistleblower record, or the 2013 Treasury-SEC PREPA communication. The official April 13, 2016 hearing transcript reviewed for this report likewise presents Treasury's restructuring case without identifying those specific records. A complete omission finding across every PROMESA hearing should be supported by a transcript-by-transcript index, which can be appended to this report.

5D. Final Treasury Exhibit — The 22-Page August 2, 2013 Treasury-to-SEC Email

Source and authorship. The FOIA-produced record in the evidentiary archive is a 22-page August 2, 2013 Treasury-to-SEC communication. The opening email is from Adam Chepenik of the U.S. Treasury Department. Contemporary and later professional biographies identify Chepenik as a Treasury Senior Policy Advisor and later Deputy Director of the Office of State and Local Finance, advising the Secretary, the Under Secretary for Domestic Finance, and the Assistant Secretary for Financial Markets on distressed municipalities including Puerto Rico.

What the record shows. The email was sent before the 2013 PREPA bond issuance and transmitted market and transaction concerns to SEC personnel. The archive quotes Chepenik apologizing for the length of the email, noting accelerating municipal-market outflows and volatility, and directing the SEC to materials describing what Bloomberg, Reuters, and rating agencies were saying about the transaction. The larger 22-page record is cited in the docketed evidentiary materials as Treasury personnel actively identifying and analyzing PREPA/Puerto Rico bond risks rather than merely receiving information from the SEC.

Why its origin matters. Chepenik was not an outside commentator. He was a Treasury policy official working within the domestic-finance structure that advised senior Treasury leadership. Later accounts identify Chepenik as playing a significant role in Treasury's Puerto Rico response and in technical assistance to Congress during PROMESA. This creates a direct institutional continuity: a Treasury official involved in PREPA analysis in 2013 later participated in the Treasury apparatus that helped develop the federal Puerto Rico response.

Congressional omission question. The central issue is not whether the 22-page email itself proves securities fraud. It is whether the materially specific PREPA risk analysis already circulating between Treasury and the SEC was disclosed to Congress when Treasury later helped frame the factual basis for PROMESA. Lawless states that his review of the hearing transcripts found no such disclosure. The official April 2016 hearing record reviewed for this report does not identify the 22-page email, Chepenik's 2013 PREPA analysis, B-02949, HR 1049, or the whistleblower materials in Treasury's presentation.

Investigative significance. If Treasury's congressional briefing books and draft testimony confirm that the 2013 PREPA analysis and related SEC investigative information were available internally but omitted from the legislative presentation, investigators should determine who made that decision, whether the omission was considered material, and whether conflicts involving Citigroup, Lazard, creditor interests, or other financial stakeholders influenced the scope of what Congress was told.

5E. SEC Leadership After PROMESA — Gary Gensler / Goldman Sachs / FOIA-Era PREPA Records

Background and financial-sector relationship. Gary Gensler served as SEC Chair from April 17, 2021 through January 20, 2025. Before entering government, he spent 18 years at Goldman Sachs, became a partner, headed the firm's Media Group, led fixed-income and currency trading in Asia, and became co-head of Finance with responsibility for worldwide Controllers and Treasury functions. He later served as Treasury Under Secretary for Domestic Finance and as CFTC Chair before assuming leadership of the SEC.

Why his tenure matters to PREPA. Gensler did not preside over the original 2013 PREPA bond issuance or the enactment of PROMESA. His relevance is different: he inherited the SEC after the PREPA investigative record, B-02949, whistleblower materials, subpoenas, and interagency records had already accumulated, and he became Chair just as FOIA requests and related litigation began forcing the agency to locate and process the historical PREPA/Lawless record.

FOIA chronology. Gensler was sworn in on April 17, 2021. The SEC received Lawless's FOIA request for internal communications concerning him on May 19, 2021—approximately one month later. In its September 24, 2021 response, the SEC quoted Lawless's request for records concerning the whistleblower, criminal complaints, and the hundreds of emails, faxes, and Federal Express packages concerning Puerto Rico bankruptcy and alleged bond fraud. The SEC stated that processing involved potentially voluminous responsive records, records located in geographically separated components, and consultation with one or more other offices having a substantial interest in the determination or subject matter.

Investigative significance. The key question for the Gensler period is therefore what information reached the Chair's office about the scope and sensitivity of the PREPA record once the SEC was required to search for it. The currently assembled evidence does not establish that Gensler personally reviewed B-02949, the Lawless submissions, or the agency's litigation positions. It does establish a period in which his SEC was actively processing requests that implicated a substantial historical investigative record.

Goldman-related conflict question. Because Gensler spent 18 years at Goldman Sachs and held senior positions there, investigators should obtain the ethics and recusal records applicable to any Puerto Rico/PREPA matter involving Goldman or related former-employer interests. The purpose is not to infer misconduct from prior employment, but to determine who had decisional authority over PREPA-related enforcement, FOIA, and litigation matters when a senior official had a significant former-employer relationship.

Lost text-message records. The SEC later notified the National Archives that some text messages from Gensler's SEC-issued phone were lost for the period October 18, 2022 through September 6, 2023. The SEC OIG subsequently concluded that avoidable information-technology errors caused the inadvertent loss. There is presently no evidence that the missing texts concerned PREPA, Puerto Rico, B-02949, Lawless, or the related litigation. Because the loss overlaps the broader FOIA/litigation period, however, any records-reconstruction effort should determine whether PREPA-related searches, litigation holds, or responsive custodians encompassed those missing messages.

7. The Congressional Disclosure Question

The central oversight question is therefore not whether Congress was generally aware that Puerto Rico was in fiscal distress. It plainly was. The question is whether Congress was informed of the materially more specific federal record concerning PREPA.

  • Did Treasury's PROMESA briefing books disclose the 2013 Treasury-to-SEC PREPA analysis?

  • Did Treasury tell the House Natural Resources Committee that SEC Enforcement Matter B-02949 existed?

  • Did congressional testimony disclose that the SEC had sought evidence concerning PREPA debt-service capacity, receivables, subsidies, solvency, continuing disclosure, and offering assumptions?

  • Did Treasury disclose SEC-DOJ investigative communications concerning PREPA?

  • Did committee members receive the whistleblower and investigative information before relying on Treasury’s description of the causes and proposed solution to Puerto Rico’s debt crisis?

The available public congressional record and Treasury testimony reviewed for this report emphasize the unsustainable aggregate debt, liquidity crisis, creditor litigation, restructuring authority, and oversight. They do not themselves demonstrate that the PREPA-specific investigative record described above was presented to Congress.

8. Why Omission of PREPA-Specific Information Could Have Been Material

PROMESA was premised on a congressional diagnosis of Puerto Rico’s fiscal crisis and a statutory solution built around restructuring and oversight. PREPA was one of the major public debt issuers ultimately placed within that framework.

If Congress had been told that federal regulators had already developed evidence potentially calling into question bond disclosures, debt-service assumptions, solvency representations, or continuing-disclosure practices, that information could have affected at least four legislative judgments:

  • whether the crisis was principally one of excessive public borrowing versus potentially actionable securities misconduct;

  • whether creditor claims should enter a restructuring process without first resolving threshold validity or fraud issues;

  • whether federal investigative findings should be preserved, disclosed, or referred before imposing a broad restructuring framework;

  • whether special protections, investigative mechanisms, or disclosure requirements should have been included in PROMESA.

This is a materiality argument, not a claim that Congress necessarily would have rejected PROMESA.

9. Later SEC and DOJ Conduct as Potential Corroborating Evidence

The later-produced Curtin records add a second phase to the influence theory. The user’s consolidated evidentiary materials identify 39 challenged government statements or characterizations across related litigation. The report appropriately distinguishes those statements from 39 separate crimes: some are advocacy, omissions, shorthand, or disputed characterizations.

What the later record does establish, according to the docketed evidentiary materials, is that the Government possessed contemporaneous corroborating records while related matters were later characterized as unsupported. The 447-page Curtin production documents whistleblower processing, investigative work items, requests for analysis, preservation, and SEC-DOJ communications.

The counsel-notice chronology also matters. A third-party review in the evidence archive states that SEC/PREPA investigative information and the existence of SEC-DOJ communications were placed on a federal docket and served on DOJ litigation counsel in May and June 2022. This does not prove what any attorney actually knew, but it makes the question objectively discoverable through DOJ litigation files and internal communications.

Investigative significance: If federal agencies repeatedly defended positions that prevented discovery or merits adjudication after receiving notice of records that materially complicated those positions, investigators could examine whether the conduct reflected ordinary litigation defense, institutional compartmentalization, or a broader effort to preserve the PROMESA-era narrative and restructuring framework.

11. The Missing Causal Bridge

The theory becomes substantially stronger if investigators find evidence connecting congressional financial relationships to agency conduct. The most important missing evidence is not more contribution data. It is communications.

  • Emails, texts, memoranda, or meeting notes between congressional leadership/staff and Treasury, SEC, or DOJ concerning PREPA investigations, bond validity, whistleblower submissions, or disclosure to Congress.

  • Treasury briefing books and draft testimony showing whether PREPA-specific investigative information was included, removed, softened, or excluded.

  • SEC or DOJ communications discussing congressional sensitivities, major creditors, underwriting banks, PROMESA, or the consequences of enforcement action for the restructuring.

  • Bank, trade-association, hedge-fund, insurer, or law-firm communications with members/staff followed by government contacts concerning the same PREPA issues.

  • Evidence that officials were instructed to defer, narrow, close, withhold, or characterize investigative information for political or legislative reasons.

  • Board-seat, employment, stock, compensation, fundraising, or other benefit evidence temporally linked to identifiable official acts, if such records exist.

Those are the records capable of converting an inference of political protection into evidence of an agreement or corrupt purpose.

13. Findings

  • Finding 1 — Financial motive and access are documented. Major financial institutions and securities interests had both economic stakes in Puerto Rico debt and extraordinary political/lobbying capacity.

  • Finding 2 — Political gatekeepers with substantial financial-sector support controlled key PROMESA stages. This supports scrutiny of influence but does not prove corrupt action.

  • Finding 3 — Treasury was a co-developer and principal Administration advocate of the congressional solution, not a detached observer.

  • Finding 4 — Treasury, SEC, and DOJ-related records establish PREPA-specific federal knowledge and interagency activity predating or overlapping the PROMESA process.

  • Finding 5 — The public congressional record reviewed here does not itself show that the specific SEC investigative record was disclosed to Congress.

  • Finding 6 — Later SEC/DOJ records materially complicate categorical claims that the PREPA allegations were unsupported and create a separate question about institutional preservation of earlier positions.

  • Finding 7 — Taken together, motive, access, legislative control, executive-branch knowledge, interagency contacts, and later protective litigation outcomes create a reasonable basis for investigating political influence.

  • Finding 8 — The current evidence does not yet establish the direct instruction, agreement, or quid pro quo needed to state that politicians actually ordered federal agencies to protect bank contributors.

14. Conclusion

The strongest version of the political-influence claim is not that campaign contributions alone caused PROMESA. The record is more complex and, in important respects, more consequential.

The evidence shows a convergence of interests: major financial institutions and securities interests had enormous financial exposure and political reach; important congressional gatekeepers received substantial financial-sector support; industry and creditor interests were actively lobbying over Puerto Rico; Treasury worked directly with congressional committees to design and promote PROMESA; and Treasury, SEC, and DOJ-related records indicate that the Executive Branch possessed a substantially more detailed PREPA evidentiary record than appears in the public legislative narrative.

The later litigation record supplies a potential continuation theory: after PROMESA established the restructuring framework, later-produced government records allegedly contradicted or materially complicated factual positions used to defeat litigation and prevent discovery into the underlying PREPA issues. If proven intentional and connected to political direction, that sequence could support a much more serious inference of institutional protection.

But the report should stop at the evidentiary line. No document presently identified here contains a direct order from Schumer, Ryan, McConnell, Duffy, Bishop, or another politician instructing SEC, DOJ, or Treasury to conceal PREPA evidence or protect a contributor. That missing causal bridge is why the proper conclusion is that the claim is sufficiently grounded to warrant subpoenas and investigation—not that the corrupt agreement has already been proven.

Bottom line: The current record supports a serious investigation into whether politically powerful recipients of financial-sector support helped create and preserve a federal restructuring framework favorable to institutional financial interests and whether that political environment influenced the Executive Branch’s handling and disclosure of PREPA-related evidence.
